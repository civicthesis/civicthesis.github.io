The humidity of the night was thick enough to swallow a sickle, and Mohan was dreaming of a world where revolutionary pamphlets didn’t give him paper cuts. A sudden, icy draft cut through the mosquito net, and the smell of expensive Georgian tobacco filled his small room.

Mohan blinked his eyes open. Sitting on his only plastic chair—the one with the cracked leg—was a man with a mustache so thick it looked like it could command its own battalion.

### The Midnight Guest

"Who are you?" Mohan stammered, clutching his lungi. "If you’re from the electricity board, I paid the bill last Tuesday."

The man leaned forward, his eyes like two cold coals. "I am Joseph Vissarionovich Stalin. General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union. The Architect of Socialism. The Man of Steel."

Mohan rubbed his eyes. "Right. And I’m the Maharaja of Air India. Look, Comrade, it’s 2:00 AM. If you want a donation for the party, come back after sunrise. And lose the costume; the mustache is a bit much, even for a local theater production."

Stalin didn't smile. He gestured toward the window. "You doubt the steel? Look outside, little worker."

### A View of the Red Army

Mohan crawled to the window and pulled back the faded curtain. His jaw hit the windowsill.

In the narrow, dusty lane of his Indian village, where usually only a few stray cows and a broken bicycle resided, stood a line of T-34 tanks. Thousands of soldiers in heavy wool greatcoats—entirely impractical for the tropical heat—stood in perfect formation, their bayonets gleaming under the flickering streetlamp.

"Is that... proof enough?" Stalin asked, his voice a low rumble.

Mohan collapsed back onto his bed, trembling. "General Secretary! I—I thought you were dead. Or at least, you know, in Moscow. In a jar."

"Death is a bourgeois construct," Stalin said, sitting back. "Now, sit. We have to discuss your itinerary for tomorrow."

### The Statue Dilemma

Stalin leaned in, the smell of tobacco growing stronger. "I hear rumors, Mohan. I hear the Communist Party of India wants to be... *democratic*. I hear you are going to burn a statue tomorrow. My statue."

"It’s a symbolic gesture, Comrade!" Mohan squeaked. "To protest dictatorship! To say no to violence! We want a kinder, gentler communism. You know, with more voting and fewer... well, fewer 'disappearances.'"

Stalin sighed, a sound like a sliding tombstone.

> "Why me, Mohan? Why always the mustache? There are so many dictators in history. Why don’t you burn a statue of Hitler? Or Mussolini? Or at least a very tall, very rude landlord? Why must my bronze nose be melted down for 'democracy'?"

"It’s about the message—"

"The message is loud," Stalin interrupted. "But why join this protest? Why not go to the beach? Or stay home and read a book about heavy industry? Burning statues is a messy business. It gets soot on your clothes."

### The Final Warning

Stalin stood up. He seemed to grow taller, his shadow stretching across the ceiling until it covered the entire room. He placed a heavy, cold hand on Mohan’s shoulder.

"Listen to me, little worker. If you go tomorrow, and if that match touches my bronze feet... if you burn the man who turned Russia from a plow to an atomic bomb..."

He leaned into Mohan’s ear and spoke a sentence in thick, guttural Russian:
**"Если ты это сделаешь, я приду за твоим завтраком, а потом за твоей душой, и в Сибири всегда есть место для одного маленького индийца."**

"What? What does that mean?" Mohan gasped, his heart hammering against his ribs. "I don't speak Russian! What will you do?"

Stalin didn't answer. He simply turned and walked through the wall as if it were smoke. Outside, the roar of tank engines ignited, a deafening sound that shook the very foundations of the house.

### The Awakening

"Wait! What will you do?!" Mohan screamed.

He jolted upright. The room was silent. The smell of tobacco was gone, replaced by the faint scent of mosquito coils. The plastic chair was empty. Through the window, the lane was empty—no tanks, no Red Army, just a lone dog barking at a moonbeam.

Mohan sat in the dark, sweating, wondering if he should check the local scrap yard to see if the statue was still there, or if he should just call in sick to the revolution tomorrow.
