Mohan turned in his sleep, the thin mat on the floor offering little comfort against the humid night air of Kerala. His small room, usually filled with the sound of crickets, was unnervingly silent.

He opened his eyes.

A figure sat in the cane chair by the window. A heavy silhouette against the moonlight. The smell of pipe tobacco, rich and foreign, cut through the scent of stale sweat and mosquito coils.

"Comrade," a deep voice rumbled.

Mohan sat up, groping for his glasses on the bedside stool. "Who is there? How did you get in?"

The figure leaned forward, the moonlight catching the thick, familiar mustache and the shock of brushed-back hair. It was a face Mohan had seen on crucial posters in the party office since he was a boy, a face that had stared down from banners for decades.

"Joseph Vissarionovich?" Mohan whispered, his voice trembling. Then, skepticism washed over him. He was a modern party worker, rational and scientific. "No. No, this is a trick. Who are you? Some actor sent by the opposition to mock us?"

The man chuckled, a sound like grinding stones. "You require proof, Comrade Mohan. Good. A materialist always demands evidence."

Joseph Stalin stood up, his military tunic shifting with heavy fabric sounds that shouldn't exist in the heat, and gestured to the window. "Look."

Mohan hesitated, then crept to the window. He peered out into the familiar narrow alleyway where his neighbor's scooter was usually parked.

But the alley was gone.

Instead, rank upon rank of soldiers in heavy grey greatcoats stood in the gloom, their bayonets gleaming under a cold, alien moon that looked nothing like the one over India. The Red Army, spectral and silent, filled the streets of Mohan's village as far as the eye could see. A T-34 tank sat where the tea shop should have been, its turret swiveling slowly with a mechanical groan that vibrated in Mohan's teeth.

Mohan gasped and stumbled back, falling onto his mat. When he looked up, the alley was back. The soldiers were gone. The tea shop was silent.

Stalin was sitting again, calmly repacking his pipe.

"Sit," Stalin commanded. It wasn't a request.

Mohan sat, his legs weak.

"I hear," Stalin said, lighting a match that flared with impossible brightness, "that the party—*my* party's Indian branch—is planning a spectacle tomorrow."

Mohan swallowed hard. "It... it is a protest, Comrade. We are marching to the town square."

"To burn an effigy," Stalin stated flatly. "Of me."

Mohan looked down at his hands, ashamed but defiant. "To say no to dictatorship. To say no to violence within the movement. We must promote democracy, Comrade. We must show the people we have changed."

Stalin puff on his pipe. Smoke curled into shapes that looked like maps of fallen empires. "Democracy," he mused. "A pretty word. Tell me, Mohan, why me? Am I the only ghost you fear? Why do you not burn the statue of other dictators? Why not the imperialists who starve your people? Why not join the other protests that fight for bread and water?"

"It is... symbolic," Mohan stammered. "We must clean our own house first. We must reject the past errors."

"Cleaning the house by burning the roof?" Stalin scowled. "You wish to show you are modern. You wish to appease the liberals. So you sacrifice the old symbol."

He leaned in close, his eyes yellow and piercing. "But be warned, Comrade. Symbols have weight. History is heavy. It does not burn."

Stalin stood up, towering over the small worker. "If you burn my statue tomorrow..."

He paused, the shadows in the room seeming to lengthen and grab at Mohan's ankles.

"...*Esli ty sozhzhesh' menya, ty sozhzhesh' i sebya. Istoriya ne proshchayet trusosti.*"

Mohan stared, uncomprehending. The Russian words felt like heavy stones dropping into a deep well. "What? What did you say? I don't understand!"

Stalin didn't answer. He turned his back and walked toward the door, dissolving into the shadows before he even reached the frame.

"Wait!" Mohan shouted. "What will happen?"

"Mohan? Mohan!"

Mohan jolted awake. His wife, Lakshmi, was shaking his shoulder. "Mohan! You were shouting. A bad dream?"

Mohan looked around the empty room. Sunlight was streaming through the window. The cane chair was empty, piled with yesterday's clothes. The smell of tobacco was gone, replaced by the morning aroma of cardamom tea.

"Yes," Mohan breathed, wiping cold sweat from his forehead. "Just a dream. A very strange dream."

He got up to wash his face. As he walked past the window, he glanced at the floor where the figure had sat.

There, on the clean tile, lay a small pile of grey ash, still warm, in the shape of a pipe.
