At thirty-two, Jay Gupta possessed a Master’s degree in Civil Engineering, a checking account that frequently echoed with the hollow sound of overdraft fees, and a virginity so entrenched it had practically achieved historical landmark status.

He knew a great deal about static loads, concrete curing rates, and the theoretical shear strength of a bridge abutment. He knew absolutely nothing about things that moved, got hot, or required grease.

His mid-life crisis arrived early one Tuesday at 3:00 AM, illuminated by the blue light of his laptop amidst empty ramen cups. He was watching a video of an F-16 engine test. The noise—a visceral, hateful scream of power—spoke to him. It was everything he wasn't: loud, hot, and capable of rapid acceleration.

"I," Jay announced to an indifferent cockroach on his wall, "am going to build a turbojet engine."

He didn't have a garage. He didn't have tools. He didn't know the difference between a bolt and a machine screw. But he had the desperate, manic energy of a man who hadn't touched a woman since the Bush administration.

### Phase 1: The Metallurgy Misunderstanding

Jay’s first foray was to the local hardware mega-store. He approached an elderly employee named Bob, whose apron looked like a crime scene of spilled paint.

"Excuse me," Jay said, adjusting his glasses. "I require metal capable of withstanding two thousand degrees Celsius and extreme centrifugal forces. Also, what is 'welding'?"

Bob blinked slowly. "Aisle 14. Galvanized pipe. Don't breathe the fumes if you heat it."

Jay bought four feet of galvanized plumbing pipe, a hacksaw, and a suspiciously cheap stick welder made in a country that no longer existed.

Back in his 300-square-foot studio apartment, Jay prepared to weld the combustion chamber. He had watched a four-minute YouTube tutorial titled "Welding is E-Z!" He decided safety goggles were a suggestion of the nanny state.

He struck the arc.

It was the brightest light Jay had ever seen, including the time he stared directly at the sun hoping for superpowers. He managed to stick the electrode to the pipe, creating a shower of sparks that ignited his curtains.

The real fun started four hours later, when the "arc eye" set in. It felt as though someone had replaced his contact lenses with crushed glass and Tabasco sauce. He spent the next two days lying in the dark with frozen peas on his face, listening to an audiobook of Stephen Hawking’s *A Brief History of Time* to feel intellectually superior to his agonizing corneas.

**Lessons Learned:** The existence of UV radiation. The melting point of synthetic curtains. The fact that galvanized metal releases toxic zinc oxide fumes when heated, giving him a mild case of metal fume fever that felt like the flu laced with regret.

### Phase 2: The Plastic Pivot

Realizing that molten metal was perhaps too advanced for a man who struggled with child-proof caps, Jay pivoted.

"Aerospace is moving toward composites anyway," he rationalized, maxing out his second credit card on a budget 3D printer.

He decided to print the compressor stage. He needed a complex, multi-bladed fan that could spin at 50,000 RPM. He downloaded a file from a Russian forum that translated roughly to "Spinny Death Wheel v4."

Jay knew nothing about polymers. He bought the cheapest PLA plastic available—a material derived from cornstarch that begins to droop if left in a warm car.

For three weeks, Jay’s apartment sounded like a robotic dentist’s office. The printer failed spectacularly. It created spaghetti monsters of filament. It clogged. It jammed.

Jay, fueled by spite and cheap energy drinks, learned. He learned about bed leveling, nozzle temperatures, and G-code. He learned that physics hates amateur engineers.

Finally, he produced a beautiful, silver-painted compressor wheel. It looked like the future.

To test it, he attached it to a Dremel tool and cranked it to maximum speed.

The cornstarch plastic fan reached roughly 20,000 RPM before physics stepped in to collect its due. The centrifugal force overcame the pathetic layer adhesion of the PLA.

The fan exploded.

It wasn't a fiery explosion; it was a shrapnel event. A shard of silver plastic embedded itself half an inch into the drywall next to Jay’s head. Another piece sliced a neat line across his thigh, missing his femoral artery by a margin that proved God has a dark sense of humor.

Jay sat on the floor, bleeding and looking at the piece of plastic sticking out of his wall.

**Lessons Learned:** Material science is not optional. Centrifugal force is a bitch. How to perform minor surgery on oneself using tweezers and vodka.

### Phase 3: The Bernoulli Obsession

Bedridden with a limp and zinc poisoning residue, Jay realized he didn’t actually know *how* a jet engine worked. He thought you just squeezed air and lit it on fire.

He dove into fluid dynamics. He stopped bathing. He grew a beard that caught crumbs. He sat surrounded by textbooks, muttering about Bernoulli's principle and the Brayton cycle.

He began to see the world in terms of pressure differentials. He tried to explain to his landlord that the reason the toilet wouldn't flush was due to inadequate boundary layer control in the U-bend.

"Jay," the landlord sighed, sliding an eviction notice under the door, "just pay the rent."

He learned thermodynamics. He realized why his galvanized pipe idea was suicidal. He realized why his plastic fan was idiotic. He realized he was the engineering equivalent of a toddler trying to fly a 747.

But he understood *why*.

### The Final Test

Desperate, facing homelessness, and still tragically un-laid, Jay decided on one final attempt. He abandoned the high-tech approach.

He went to a junkyard and scavenged a turbocharger from a rusted-out Volvo station wagon. He didn't know what it was, just that it spun freely. He hooked the compressor side to a leaf blower (for starting air) and the turbine side to a combustion chamber he made from a modified propane tank he stole from a neighbor's barbecue.

He set it up in the communal alleyway at 2:00 AM, strapped to a stolen shopping cart.

It looked like a pipe bomb designed by Salvador Dalí.

Jay stood behind a sheet of plywood, wearing three pairs of sunglasses taped together. He turned on the leaf blower. The turbo whined. He cracked the propane valve. He hit the barbecue igniter.

*WHOOSH-KA-CHUG-CHUG-SCREAM.*

It worked. Sort of.

It didn't produce thrust so much as it produced noise and terrifying heat. A three-foot tongue of orange flame shot out the back, incinerating the neighbor’s prize-winning hydrangeas. The turbocharger, never designed for this abuse, began to glow cherry red. The sound was incredible—a high-pitched shriek of mechanical agony that woke the entire block.

For twelve glorious seconds, Jay Gupta was an aerospace engineer.

Then the oil seals in the borrowed turbo failed. Thick white smoke billowed out, immediately followed by the turbine wheel seizing, shearing off its shaft, and exiting the rear of the engine at ballistic speeds, embedding itself in a dumpster.

The fire department arrived four minutes later.

### The Aftermath

Jay sat in an interview chair two weeks later. He was wearing a suit he bought at Goodwill that smelled faintly of embalming fluid. He was interviewing for a junior drafting position at a company that manufactured industrial sewage pumps.

The interviewer, a stern woman named Brenda, looked at his resume. "It says here you're a civil engineer. Why do you want to draw sewage pumps?"

"I need the money," Jay said honestly. "I owe the city for a fire response, my landlord for damages, and the hardware store for a lot of galvanized pipe."

Brenda raised an eyebrow. "Explain."

Jay, having nothing left to lose, told her everything. The zinc poisoning. The arc eye that left him blind for two days. The cornstarch shrapnel that scarred his inner thigh. The deep dive into Bernoulli’s principle while high on pain meds. The final, glorious, twelve-second ignition of the shopping cart jet that murdered the hydrangeas.

He spoke with passion about compressor stall and stoichiometric ratios. He detailed the exact metallurgic failure point of the Volvo turbocharger.

Brenda stared at him for a long, uncomfortable silence.

"You're an idiot, Mr. Gupta," she said finally. "A deeply dangerous, reckless idiot who should probably be on a watchlist."

Jay slumped. "I know."

"But," she tapped his resume, "you learned thermodynamics, CAD modeling, 3D printing, and practical metallurgy in six months while poisoning yourself in a studio apartment. Most of my interns can't operate the coffee machine without supervision."

Jay looked up.

"You're hired," Brenda sighed. "You start Monday. Don't ever touch the real machinery."

Jay didn't build a jet engine. He was still broke, still renting, and still a virgin. But as he walked out of the office, gainfully employed as a designer of feces-moving technology, he felt a strange sense of thrust.
